Two passengers on the same flight, sitting next to each other, paid different prices for identical seats. One paid three times what the other did. Neither was cheated. They were sorted.

Price discrimination has a bad reputation because the word sounds like a scam. But it is one of the most ordinary facts of commercial life: **firms charge what different customers are willing to pay**, and almost every industry has found a way to do it — sometimes crudely, sometimes with remarkable precision.

## The three degrees

Economists usually split it into three flavours:

- **First degree** — charge each customer their exact willingness to pay. Practically impossible; you would need to read minds.
- **Second degree** — let customers sort themselves through menus: bulk discounts, premium tiers, advance-purchase fares.
- **Third degree** — charge different groups different prices: student discounts, regional pricing, business vs. leisure travellers.

Third degree is the one people notice, because it is visible and sometimes uncomfortable. Second degree is the one that quietly shapes most consumer markets.

## Why it can be good

Textbook economics says a monopolist who price-discriminates may serve more customers than one who charges a single price. The single-price monopolist leaves out everyone whose willingness to pay is below the price but above marginal cost. A menu of prices can capture some of those customers without giving up revenue from the high end.

The result is a genuine trade-off:

| | Single price | Discriminatory pricing |
|---|---|---|
| Output | Lower | Higher |
| Firm profit | Lower | Higher |
| Consumer surplus | Split unevenly | Split unevenly |
| Fairness perception | Simpler | Contentious |

Whether the trade-off is acceptable is a political question, not an arithmetic one. But it is not true that discrimination is always welfare-destroying. Often it expands access — the cheap student ticket exists because the expensive flexible fare subsidises it.

## The mechanism is the product

The interesting business insight is that price discrimination is a **design** problem. Firms do not simply wish for different prices; they build structures that make customers reveal their type:

- Saturday-night stay requirements separate business from leisure travellers.
- Versioning (basic/pro/enterprise) makes willingness to pay legible.
- Waiting in a queue is a price, paid in time rather than money — which is why "free with ads" tiers work.

Each mechanism is a sorting device. The firm is not just selling a product; it is selling a product **plus a way to identify who you are.**

## Where it goes wrong

Three failure modes recur:

1. **Arbitrage.** If the cheap version is substitutable for the expensive one, the sorting collapses. Region-free software keys, resale markets, and shared streaming passwords are all arbitrage.
2. **Fairness backlash.** Customers tolerate being sorted by behaviour (you chose to book late) far more than by identity (you are from this postcode). The legal and reputational line runs through perceived justification, not economic logic.
3. **Optimisation against the metric.** Personalised pricing can cross into targeting that feels like surveillance, even when it is technically legal. The long-run cost is trust, which is hard to put in a spreadsheet.

## Takeaway

Price discrimination is not a moral failure of markets; it is what markets do when information allows. The honest questions are: **who is being sorted, on what basis, and who bears the cost of being wrong?** Those questions are answerable. "It's just supply and demand" is not an answer — it is a way of skipping them.
